    <p><strong>Renogy</strong> consolidated its rigid-panel range around N-Type cells on <strong>12 June</strong>, launching a <strong>bifacial series</strong> in <strong>100W, 200W and 400W</strong> ratings (a 320W variant is also listed) to join the 250W and 590W bifacial panels already on the books. The move follows the wider industry shift away from p-type PERC toward <strong>N-Type TOPCon</strong> cells, which run a little more efficiently, degrade more slowly, and hold output better as they heat up — all of which matters on a roof that bakes in the sun all day.</p>
    <p>The headline number is bifaciality. Renogy rates the cells at roughly <strong>25% efficiency</strong> on a <strong>16-busbar</strong> layout and claims the glass-backed panels generate about <strong>14% more energy</strong> from reflected light hitting the rear, citing an <strong>80% bifaciality factor</strong> against traditional PERC. The company frames the series for "off-grid and distributed energy" — RV, overland, marine and remote-infrastructure use — and lists the 100W from around <strong>$106</strong>, with pricing scaling by wattage. "At Renogy, we believe every ray of sunlight carries value — and every inch of space should work," the company said in its launch release.</p>
    <p>Two caveats worth keeping in front of the marketing. First, the rear-side gain is real only when light can reach the back of the panel: a module flush-mounted on a white RV roof sees very little of it, while a tilted ground array over gravel or snow sees a lot. Second, for most mobile installs the genuine upgrade here is the front side — higher efficiency per square foot and a better temperature coefficient — not the bifacial bonus. Renogy was an early mover putting bifacial panels into mobile setups back in 2023; this is the company standardising on the cell technology rather than a new trick. We dig into whether bifacial pays off on a van roof below.</p>

    body: |
      <p><strong>Marine How To</strong> published a useful reminder on <strong>20 June</strong>: the marks stamped on budget DC gear are not the same as having been tested. The piece centres on the genuine <strong>CE</strong> (Conformité Européenne) mark and its near-twin, an unofficial "<strong>China Export</strong>" logo with the letters spaced more tightly together, which looks almost identical but certifies nothing. The author names the usual suspects — <strong>inverters, MPPT charge controllers, battery chargers and diesel heaters</strong> — as the products where the fake mark turns up most.</p>
      <p>The argument is simple and worth internalising before the next cut-price Amazon purchase: "a logo is not evidence." What is evidence, per the article, is a <strong>Declaration of Conformity</strong> you can actually read, technical specifications, <strong>EMC test reports</strong>, an identifiable manufacturer with a physical address, and support you can reach. None of that is a guarantee either, but it's a far better filter than a symbol anyone can print. This is opinion from a respected marine-electrical source rather than a regulatory notice — but it's the kind of opinion that saves a cabin fire.</p>

# ── The Background ──
background:
  type: market_analysis
  headline: "Does bifacial solar actually pay off on a van roof?"
  body: |
    <p>This week's Renogy launch leans hard on bifaciality — the panel's ability to make power from light hitting its rear. It's a real effect, and on the right mount it's worth money. On the wrong mount, which describes most RV roofs, it's close to free marketing. Here's how to tell which one you have.</p>
    <p>A bifacial panel is rated by two numbers: its front-side wattage, and a <strong>bifaciality factor</strong> — the fraction of front efficiency the rear cells deliver under equal light. Renogy quotes <strong>80%</strong>, which is typical for a good N-Type module. But that factor only describes the cells' <em>potential</em>. The actual rear-side yield is governed by how much light reaches the back, and that comes down to three things: the <strong>albedo</strong> (reflectivity) of whatever is behind the panel, the <strong>air gap</strong> between panel and surface, and how much of the rear is <strong>shaded</strong> by mounting rails, the roof itself, or the structure underneath.</p>
    <h3>The flush-mount problem</h3>
    <p>Stick a bifacial panel flat against a van or RV roof on low-profile brackets and you have defeated the feature. The rear faces a surface a few centimetres away, in its own shadow, with almost no light getting in from the sides. Real-world rear-side contribution in that configuration is typically in the low single digits of percent — sometimes effectively zero. You paid for glass on the back and a heavier panel, and you're running it as a monofacial module. A white roof helps a little by bouncing some light into the gap; a dark roof helps not at all.</p>
    <h3>Where it does pay</h3>
    <p>Bifacial gain shows up when the panel is <strong>tilted and raised</strong> over a reflective surface. A ground-deployed array on a portable stand over light gravel, concrete or sand can pick up <strong>5–15%</strong>; over fresh snow, considerably more. Tilt legs on a roof rack that lift the panel several inches and angle it toward the sun also open up the rear to ambient and reflected light. The pattern is consistent: the more the panel behaves like a free-standing module with sky and bright ground behind it, the more the second number on the datasheet turns into real amps.</p>
    <p>So is the new Renogy series worth it for a mobile build? Possibly — but mostly for the <strong>front side</strong>. N-Type TOPCon cells give you higher efficiency per square foot (useful when roof space is the binding constraint), a flatter temperature coefficient (useful when the panel is pinned to a hot roof), and slower long-term degradation. Those benefits apply regardless of mounting. The bifacial bonus is a genuine extra only if you tilt, lift, or ground-deploy. Buy the panel for the cells; treat the rear-side number as a bonus you have to earn with your mounting, not a spec you automatically collect.</p>
