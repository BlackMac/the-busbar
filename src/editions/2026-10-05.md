# ── The Lead ──
lead:
  tag: Safety
  headline: "Grand Design recalls 16,128 Reflection fifth wheels: the solar controller can pick 24V output on a 12V trailer"
  tl_dr: "On some 2024–2027 Reflections, the 12V/24V auto-detect solar controller can choose the wrong system voltage and damage the 7-way bulkhead power center. Brakes and exterior lights hang off that circuit. The fix is a software update; owner letters go out 16 November."
  body: |
    <p>On <strong>24 September</strong> Grand Design RV filed NHTSA recall <strong>#26V616000</strong> (Grand Design #910068) covering <strong>16,128</strong> 2024–2027 <strong>Reflection</strong> fifth-wheel trailers. NHTSA's summary: "The auto detect solar controller may select a 24 volt output when the electrical system is designed for 12 volts. The incorrect voltage may damage the 7-way bulkhead power center and other circuits."</p>
    <p>There are two consequences. A damaged power center is a fire risk. And because the 7-way bulkhead serves the trailer's running gear, damage there "may cause the trailer brakes or exterior lightings to fail, increasing the risk of a crash." Only trailers built with <em>both</em> a 12V/24V auto-detect controller and the 7-way bulkhead power center are included. Units with a controller that has no 24V output mode are not.</p>
    <p>The remedy is "an over-the-air (OTA) software update", plus a free inspection of the lighting and repair where needed. Owner letters are due <strong>16 November 2026</strong>. Grand Design customer service is on 1-574-825-9679. The filing does not say how the update reaches a trailer. Nor does it name the controller supplier, and we have not identified it: Grand Design's own solar FAQ says only that it fits controllers "from 25A to 50A".</p>
    <p>NHTSA also does not say what makes the controller choose 24V. Controller manuals show why the decision is fragile. A controller that guesses system voltage from what it measures at the battery terminals can guess wrong. We look at how two manufacturers handle it in this week's Background.</p>
    <p>This is the third safety campaign in our coverage where software is the fix, after <a href="/editions/2026-08-17/">Winnebago's Ekko</a> and <a href="/editions/2026-09-14/">Goal Zero's YETI 3000X</a>. The timing is awkward: RVIA began enforcing a requirement for <em>listed</em> solar charge controllers on new RVs on 1 September (News 1). A listing tests the controller. It does not test the voltage the controller decides it is in.</p>

# ── The Background ──
background:
  type: trend_piece
  headline: "System voltage should be a setting. Some controllers treat it as a guess"
  body: |
    <p>A 12V/24V auto-detect controller is designed for the moment of installation. The installer connects a battery, and the controller looks at the terminal voltage and decides what kind of system it is in. In a workshop with a known battery, that works. Grand Design's recall in this week's Lead shows the cost when the guess is wrong in the field: 16,128 trailers whose controller may decide a 12V system is 24V.</p>
    <h3>Two ways to guess</h3>
    <p>Published manuals show two different approaches. Victron's SmartSolar MPPT manual says the battery voltage "is automatically detected at the very first power-up of the solar charger" and then stored, and that "further automatic detection is disabled". It waits 10 seconds and averages the reading. If there is no battery and the controller is powered from its PV terminals, it "will default to 12V and store that". The guess happens once, and the fallback is the lower voltage.</p>
    <p>Morningstar's ProStar MPPT manual describes the other approach. In auto-detect mode, "battery voltage over 15.5V will be detected as a 24V nominal battery", and "the 12/24V auto selection is only done at start-up". Each start-up is a new decision. On 12V, 15.5V is not an impossible reading at the terminals: an equalisation or float setting gone wrong, an alternator regulator fault, or another charger on the same bus can all produce it. Morningstar's own advice is blunt: "Generally, the specific system voltage is known, and it is best to set DIPs 2,3 accordingly; the auto-detect setting should be used only in rare circumstances."</p>
    <p>We don't know which approach the controller in the Reflection uses. NHTSA's summary does not say what triggers the wrong selection, and we are not guessing. The design question applies either way.</p>
    <h3>Why a factory build is the wrong place for auto-detect</h3>
    <p>An RV builder knows its system voltage before the first wire is cut. Every Reflection in the recall population was designed for 12V. For a factory, auto-detect saves nothing: it gives a decision the engineer has already made to a measurement taken at power-up. It makes more sense for a distributor stocking one SKU for 12V and 24V customers, and that is probably why such controllers end up on build lines. The cost of that convenience falls on whatever sits downstream. Here, that is a 7-way bulkhead feeding brakes and lights.</p>
    <h3>What listing doesn't cover</h3>
    <p>RVIA's new rule (News 1) asks for a listed controller, rated at or above the array's cold-corrected V_OC and 125% of array power. Those are good requirements, and they are about the array side. A UL 1741 listing evaluates the controller as a product. It does not evaluate whether a controller that can output 24V belongs in a 12V trailer, or what it does when it guesses wrong. That is an integration decision, and integration is the builder's job.</p>
    <p>Most auto-detect controllers show what they have decided — in an app, on a display, or through DIP switches — and many let you fix the value by hand. That setting is made once, and then the controller no longer guesses.</p>
