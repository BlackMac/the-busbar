    <p><strong>FarOutRide</strong>, the Ford Transit build blog and one of our tier-A sources, published the first installment of a page it plans to keep updating: measured, not estimated, power draw for a real van's appliances. The 20 July post breaks consumption down device by device, and the numbers carry more weight than most spec-sheet estimates because they come from actual instrumentation rather than manufacturer claims.</p>
    <p>On the DC side: a 12V compressor fridge pulled <strong>522Wh</strong> on a summer day; a Starlink Mini averaged <strong>486Wh/day</strong>, split into 45–60W during boot, 22–35W active, and 15–17W idle; the roof fan ran <strong>45W</strong> at speed 10, <strong>17W</strong> at speed 5, and <strong>2W</strong> at its lowest setting. On the AC side — measured at the outlet with Zigbee smart plugs, then padded 12% for inverter loss to estimate actual battery draw — an induction-cooktop meal cost <strong>369Wh</strong> at the plug (~414Wh from the battery); toasting in the oven, <strong>127Wh</strong> (~142Wh); a full 15L water-heater cycle, <strong>980Wh</strong> (~1,100Wh); and a day of temperature maintenance on that same heater, <strong>1,386Wh</strong> (~1,552Wh) — more than the fridge and the Starlink combined.</p>
    <p>The DC figures come from a custom "Quad-Shunt" device wired into the van's EcoFlow Distribution Panel, with system-level totals tracked through the EcoFlow app and a Home Assistant Green dashboard. Daily totals and solar-harvest figures are both marked "coming soon" — the only harvest data point published so far is qualitative: winter yield runs way below summer. For anyone sizing a system off vendor datasheets instead of a real installation, this is the kind of ground-truth reference that's usually missing.</p>

  - id: news-3
    tag: Battery
    headline: "Marine How To: your BMS's charge-rate limit and its balancing limit are different numbers"
    tl_dr: "A 24 July post from Marine How To draws a distinction worth remembering: a LiFePO4 battery's charge-rate rating and its balancing capability aren't the same spec, and conflating them is why most drop-in batteries carry a conservative 0.2C charge recommendation."
    body: |
      <p>Passive balancing — the mechanism in nearly every off-the-shelf LiFePO4 pack — only activates near the top of charge, roughly <strong>3.40V per cell</strong> with a <strong>15mV</strong> differential between cells, and works by bleeding off <strong>30–100mA</strong> through a resistor: thousands of times smaller than the charge current flowing into the pack. That mismatch, not any inherent charge-rate limit of the cells, is why manufacturers recommend <strong>0.2C</strong> — a faster charge can outrun the BMS's ability to balance before cells hit their overvoltage cutoff. The practical takeaway: charging hard through the low-to-mid state of charge isn't the problem; charging hard all the way to 100% is, since that's exactly when balancing needs the most time to work.</p>

    headline: "Same MaxxAir vent-fan defect, two more RV recalls: Winnebago and Grand Design join Tiffin"
    body: |
      <p><strong>Grand Design</strong> is recalling <strong>448</strong> 2026–2027 Lineage travel trailers (NHTSA campaign <strong>#26V446</strong>) and <strong>Winnebago</strong> is recalling <strong>134</strong> 2027 Navion and View motorhomes (<strong>#26V450</strong>) — both over the same defect: a MaxxAir N-Series Maxxfan rooftop vent fan whose printed circuit board can fail mid-cycle and overheat, a fire risk. It's the same component and the same wording <a href="/editions/2026-07-13/">Tiffin used in its own recall three weeks ago</a> (#26V412, 425 units) — three separate RV builders now recalling vehicles over one supplier's fan electronics, none of which they designed themselves. Dealers will inspect and replace the circuit board free of charge. Grand Design's owner notification letters are expected 27 July; Winnebago's, 4 September.</p>
