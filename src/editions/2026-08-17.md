The DIYSolarForum thread collecting Battle Born failure reports now runs to <strong>more than a hundred posts</strong>, by Prowse's count — a number that is a forum tally, not an audited dataset, and should be read that way. Separately, he repeated the datasheet claim we <a href="/editions/2026-06-29/">reported in June</a> — that the maximum charge current changed from 37.5A to 49A one day before the suit was filed, with both files preserved. # ── The Background ──
background:
  type: standards_explainer
  headline: "Three levels of UL, and the one letter that undoes the whole claim"
  body: |
    <p>UL marks have become the main trust signal in DIY DC, and this year they're doing a lot of work: <a href="/editions/2026-07-20/">Prowse challenging LiTime over a UL 1973 claim</a>, <a href="/editions/2026-08-10/">Expion360 selling UL 1973 into Forest River motorhomes</a>, EG4 listing UL 1973 and UL 9540A on the same wall pack. A crash course Prowse published on <strong>11 August</strong> is a good excuse to separate what these actually certify, because they are not a ladder of "more certified" — they certify different objects.</p>
    <h3>Cell, pack, system</h3>
    <p><strong>Cell level</strong> is the weakest claim and the most commonly advertised. Nearly every LFP cell in circulation — including cells sold loose on Amazon and AliExpress — carries a listing. It says nothing about the pack built around it, and nothing about grade: a 2,000-cycle cell and an 8,000-cycle cell can hold the same listing. A battery advertising only cell-level certification is telling you about a component it bought, not a product it built. Prowse's harder read is that a pack with <em>no</em> certification at all often means salvaged cells with the barcodes removed.</p>
    <p><strong>Pack level</strong> is <strong>UL 1973</strong>, and this is the one that changes a purchase. It certifies the assembled battery — cells, BMS, busbars, enclosure, safety features — as tested by a laboratory. The underrated half is the factory audit: the manufacturer is inspected to confirm it keeps building the same thing. That matters in a market where budget vendors quietly swap cell suppliers and BMS models between production runs, so two packs bought six months apart aren't the same product. UL 1973 doesn't fix that by being a harder test; it fixes it by making the swap a recertification event.</p>
    <p><strong>System level</strong> is <strong>UL 9540</strong>, covering battery, inverter, and the cabling and lugs joining them. It's what grid connection generally requires, and it only means anything if the system is installed, permitted and inspected as certified — a stack of listed batteries wired badly is not a listed system.</p>
    <h3>The letter</h3>
    <p><strong>UL 9540A is not UL 9540.</strong> It is a single test method for thermal runaway fire propagation — useful, genuinely informative, and not a system certification. It appears constantly in marketing precisely because the string looks like the bigger standard with something extra on the end. Once you've noticed it you'll see it everywhere, including on packs that could not pass UL 9540.</p>
    <p>Inverters are their own track: <strong>UL 1741</strong> for basic safety, grounding and anti-islanding, with <strong>SA</strong> and <strong>SB</strong> adding grid-support behaviour. Common enough that it doesn't differentiate much, and it says nothing about firmware quality or longevity.</p>
    <h3>The 12V gap</h3>
    <p>The asymmetry worth knowing: pack-level UL 1973 is now routine on 48V server-rack batteries, including sub-$1,000 ones. On <strong>12V</strong> it stays rare and expensive — Discover and Lithionics at the top, Epoch somewhere in the middle, and not much else. So the reader most likely to be told "get a UL 1973 battery" — the van or boat builder on 12V — is shopping in the thinnest part of the market. That gap, rather than any test detail, is the practical finding here. It's also the clearest argument for 48V architectures in new builds: the certified hardware is cheaper there, not dearer.</p>
    <p>One limit on all of it. None of these standards test the thing that has caused recent grief in this newsletter — a firmware update that bricks or misreports a pack after certification. Certification is a snapshot of hardware. The software keeps moving.</p>

recalls:
  - tag: Safety
    headline: "Winnebago recalls 96 Ekko motorhomes: a phantom touchscreen input can start the generator by itself"
    body: |
      <p><strong>Winnebago</strong> is recalling <strong>96</strong> 2024–2025 <strong>Ekko</strong> motorhomes because the touchscreen display "may register unintended or phantom inputs and turn the generator on without the operator's knowledge" (NHTSA <strong>#26V498</strong>, Winnebago #208). In an enclosed space the consequence is carbon monoxide accumulation. Owners are told to park outside until the fix is applied; notification letters are expected <strong>24 August</strong>.</p>
      <p>The remedy is the part worth noting: an <strong>over-the-air software update</strong>, free, with no dealer visit. That is a first in this newsletter's recall coverage — every electrical campaign we've tracked this year has ended with a dealer replacing a part. It also states the trade plainly. Digital switching replaced a physical generator-start switch with a touch target, and a touch target has failure modes a switch does not. Getting the fix by OTA is the same architecture paying you back.</p>
    source_label: "NHTSA — Recall Campaign #26V498 (2024–2025 Winnebago Ekko)"
    source_url: "https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls?nhtsaId=26V498"

  - tag: Safety
    headline: "Forest River recalls 21 Leprechaun and Freelander motorhomes built without a house battery breaker"
    body: |
      <p><strong>Forest River</strong> is recalling <strong>21</strong> 2027 <strong>Leprechaun</strong> and <strong>Freelander</strong> motorhomes on which "a battery post mounted breaker may not be installed, leaving the power feed wire without overcurrent protection" (NHTSA <strong>#26V503</strong>, Forest River #210-2085). Dealers will fit an overcurrent protection device free of charge; owner letters were mailed <strong>3 August</strong>.</p>
      <p>Twenty-one units and an omitted part, so: small. It belongs here anyway because it is the same failure as the <a href="/editions/2026-08-10/">Grand Design inverter-prep breaker campaign</a> and the <a href="/editions/2026-07-06/">Alliance RV undersized ground cable</a> — the protection device at the battery, the one component whose absence is invisible until the fault current arrives. An unfused main feed from a lithium house bank is a short circuit waiting for a chafe point, and nothing downstream will save it.</p>
