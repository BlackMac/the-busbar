  headline: "Dragonfly Energy sues Will Prowse for trade libel over Battle Born safety videos"
  tl_dr: "On 2 June 2026, Dragonfly Energy — the maker of Battle Born Batteries — filed a trade-libel suit against YouTuber William Prowse in Nevada's Second Judicial District Court, alleging his monetised videos misrepresented the design and safety of Battle Born's 100Ah LiFePO4 battery. The complaint claims Prowse removed internal structural parts and tested damaged units outside specification, that he praised the same product before disparaging it, and that he took more than $200,000 in affiliate and advertising payments from Dragonfly over the years before publicly denying the relationship. Dragonfly seeks damages and an injunction. These are unproven allegations in a civil complaint, and the suit escalates the terminal-failure dispute we covered in April into a courtroom."
  body: |
    <p>The Battle Born terminal saga we tracked in late April has gone legal. On <strong>2 June 2026</strong>, <strong>Dragonfly Energy Holdings</strong> — parent of <strong>Battle Born Batteries</strong> — filed a trade-libel complaint against <strong>William Errol Prowse IV</strong> and Prowse Publications LLC in the <strong>Second Judicial District Court</strong> of Nevada. The dispute is the same one that started when Prowse published a series of videos arguing that the internal terminal construction of Battle Born's 100Ah LiFePO4 battery overheated and failed; Battle Born answered in March that the melting positive terminal was an intentional polymer thermal-fuse mechanism, not a defect.</p>
    <p>Dragonfly's complaint makes several specific allegations. It says Prowse presented monetised content as independent technical analysis while <strong>removing internal structural components</strong> from the batteries and running <strong>already-damaged units outside their specifications</strong>, producing what the company calls a materially inaccurate picture of the product's safety. It further alleges that Prowse had <strong>previously praised the same battery</strong> before disparaging it, and that he received <strong>more than $200,000</strong> in affiliate commissions and advertising fees tied to Battle Born products over several years — then publicly denied that financial relationship while positioning his videos as neutral. Dragonfly seeks damages and an injunction against further statements. "This lawsuit is not about silencing criticism," said chief commercial officer <strong>Wade Seaburg</strong>. "It is about accountability when what we believe are false and misleading safety claims are presented as technical fact."</p>
    <p>Two things to keep straight. First, every claim above is an <em>allegation</em> in a civil complaint that has not been tested in court; Prowse had not filed a public response as this edition went out. Second, Nevada has an anti-SLAPP statute, and the early forum debate centres on whether Prowse can use it to get the case dismissed and recover fees. Whatever the merits, a battery maker suing the DIY world's most-watched reviewer is a genuine inflection point for how independent testing works — which is the subject of this week's Background.</p>

      <p>Published the same week Dragonfly took Will Prowse to court, a <strong>Marine How To</strong> commentary (12 June) takes the opposite stance toward a critic's findings. The author — writing as an owner who bought the batteries, not a sponsored reviewer — acknowledges the quality-control problems Prowse surfaced in budget brand <strong>Wattcycle</strong>, but says they're "not personally ready to count them out just yet, because they do &amp; have listened to feedback, unlike some others." The author still recommends buying direct rather than through third-party resellers, and points to Epoch as the premium alternative. Treat this as informed opinion rather than a controlled test — but the framing matters: it's a real-world example of the iterate-don't-litigate path.</p>

# ── The Background ──
background:
  type: trend_piece
  headline: "Litigate or iterate: independent battery testing under pressure"
  body: |
    <p>This week handed us two opposite responses to the same thing — an independent critic publishing unflattering findings about a LiFePO4 battery. Dragonfly Energy took Will Prowse to court. Wattcycle, by Marine How To's account, changed its BMS, its cells and its terminal hardware. Set the specific merits aside for a moment (the lawsuit's allegations are unproven, and Marine How To's piece is opinion) and the contrast is worth sitting with, because the DIY DC world runs on independent testing more than almost any other consumer-hardware niche.</p>
    <h3>Why testers matter here</h3>
    <p>There is no UL-style gatekeeper for the drop-in LiFePO4 market. Specs on a battery box are self-reported, datasheets are thin, and the gap between a $199 pack and an $1,199 one isn't visible from the listing photos. Into that vacuum stepped a handful of independent voices — Will Prowse, Off-Grid Garage's Andy, Panbo's cycle testing, Marine How To — whose teardowns and capacity runs became the de-facto product safety layer for a generation of van and boat builders. When that layer works, it's genuinely protective: it's how the community learned which budget cells balance properly and which BMS firmware bricks on a bad flash.</p>
    <h3>The uncomfortable part: money</h3>
    <p>The Dragonfly complaint's most pointed allegation isn't about test methodology — it's about <strong>disclosure</strong>: that Prowse took six figures in affiliate and advertising revenue tied to the same product he later criticised, then denied the relationship. Whether or not that's borne out, it lands on a real tension. Almost every major DC reviewer monetises through affiliate links, and affiliate revenue flows from the products reviewed. That doesn't make a tester dishonest, but it does mean readers should know who's paid by whom, and reviewers should make that legible. The episode is a prompt to treat undisclosed or murky affiliate relationships as a credibility signal in their own right — in either direction.</p>
    <h3>The chilling-effect question</h3>
    <p>The risk a lawsuit like this raises is obvious: if testing a battery to destruction and saying so can land you in court, smaller reviewers may simply stop. That's why the anti-SLAPP angle matters. Nevada has an anti-SLAPP statute designed to let defendants dismiss suits that target protected speech on matters of public concern — and product safety is about as public-concern as consumer speech gets. If Prowse invokes it successfully, the practical message to manufacturers is that the courtroom is an expensive way to answer a teardown. If he doesn't, expect more makers to reach for legal letters.</p>
    <h3>What the reader should take from it</h3>
    <p>Two things. First, no single tester is the last word — triangulate. A finding that Prowse, Off-Grid Garage and Panbo would all reproduce is worth more than any one channel's verdict, and a manufacturer that responds with data (or with a fixed product) tells you more than one that responds with a press release or a complaint. Second, the most reassuring signal a brand can send after a bad review is the boring one Wattcycle reportedly sent: acknowledge the fault, change the hardware, and let the next teardown find the difference. Whatever a court eventually decides about Prowse and Battle Born, that's the standard the community will keep applying.</p>
